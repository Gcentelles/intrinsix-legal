How to read a jewellery listing

Almost every bad jewellery purchase comes from the same place: the listing gave a number, and the buyer assumed it meant something it did not. This is the whole of what you need to read one properly.

1. The three numbers that decide the value

Everything else is decoration. A listing that does not give you these three is not a listing, it is a photograph with a price on it.

NumberWhy it decides
Net metal weightGross weight includes the stones, and stones weigh a lot less than gold. A 12 g "gold ring with diamonds" can hold 5 g of gold.
Purity18k is 75% gold, 14k is 58.5%, 9k is 37.5%. The same 5 g is worth twice as much in 18k as in 9k.
Stone weight and qualityCarat weight alone tells you almost nothing. A 1 ct diamond ranges from about €700 to about €12,000 depending on the other three Cs.

2. Hallmarks, and what they do not mean

MarkMeaning
750 / 18K75% gold
585 / 14K58.5% gold
375 / 9K37.5% gold
925Sterling silver
950 / PT950Platinum, 95%

A hallmark is a claim, not a guarantee. In the EU it is usually reliable; from outside it often is not. The words that mean there is no meaningful gold in the piece: gold plated, gold filled, rolled gold, vermeil, gold tone, gold coloured. Vermeil is silver with a gold layer, so it is worth the silver, and silver is worth roughly one eightieth of gold by weight.

"Tested as gold" or "acid tested" means someone scratched the surface. Plating passes that test.

3. Diamonds: the only four things that matter

Carat is weight, not size, and it is the least informative of the four on its own.

CWhat moves the price
CaratPrice per carat rises faster than weight: a 1.00 ct is worth far more than two 0.50 ct.
ColourD to Z. D-F is colourless and expensive; G-J looks white in a ring; below N the yellow is visible.
ClarityFL to I3. VS and up is clean to the eye. SI2 and below is where you must look at a photo.
CutThe only C a human chose. A poor cut kills the sparkle whatever the other three say.

Lab-grown stones are chemically identical to mined ones and sell for roughly 10% to 20% of the natural price, and that gap keeps widening. A listing that says "diamond" without saying "natural" in a market where lab-grown is everywhere is a question you must ask, not an assumption you may make.

Certificates. GIA, IGI and HRD are laboratory reports on that specific stone. An "appraisal", a "valuation certificate" or an "insurance value" is a number written by the seller or by someone the seller paid. They are not the same species of document, and the second one is worth nothing to you.

4. Coloured stones, where most of the fantasy lives

Rubies, sapphires and emeralds are routinely treated, and treatment collapses the value. The words to look for, because a careful seller does use them: heated, glass-filled, lead-glass, fracture-filled, diffusion, dyed, composite, reconstituted.

A 3 ct untreated Burmese ruby is a serious stone. A 3 ct lead-glass-filled ruby is worth less than the gold around it, and both are called "3 ct ruby" in a listing.

The same applies to the word "gemstone" used vaguely. If nobody names the species, assume the cheapest one that fits the photo.

5. Red flags, in the seller's own words

  • No weight given, or only gross weight for a piece full of stones.
  • "Valued at €4,000" next to a €900 price. The valuation is the sales pitch.
  • Retail price anchoring: what a shop charges new is the top of the ladder, not the value.
  • "Gold coloured", "gold tone", "with gold" for a piece described everywhere else as gold.
  • Stone weights given as "total carat weight" for a piece with thirty small stones: thirty 0.02 ct stones are worth almost nothing, and add up to 0.60 ct.
  • No hallmark photograph, when every other angle is photographed.
  • An urgent deadline. Real value does not need one.

6. What an auction actually costs you

The hammer price is not the price. On a €1,000 hammer at a typical platform, buying inside the EU:

LineTypical
Hammer€1,000
Buyer's premium (9% to 25%)+€90 to €250
VAT on the premium+21% of that
Shipping and insurance+€15 to €60
From outside the EU: import VAT on the total, plus duty+21% and up

A lot bought outside the EU commonly lands 35% to 45% above the hammer price. That is the number to compare against the value, and it is the reason a lot that looks cheap often is not.

7. The five questions to ask before bidding

  1. What is the net metal weight, without the stones?
  2. What is the hallmark, and can I see a photograph of it?
  3. Are the diamonds natural or lab-grown, and is there a laboratory report?
  4. Have the coloured stones been treated, and how?
  5. What is the total I pay at checkout, with premium, VAT and shipping?

A seller who answers all five plainly is usually a seller worth buying from. One who answers none has told you something too.

8. Value is a ladder, not a number

The same piece is honestly worth five different numbers, and which one applies depends on who you are and what you are about to do.

RungWhat it is
IntrinsicMelt the metal, break out the stones. The floor. Nothing sells below it for long.
WholesaleWhat a dealer pays, knowing they must resell it.
Fair priceWhat a private sale between two informed people looks like. This is the number you should aim at.
Second-hand shopWhat a shop charges, having paid wholesale and added rent and risk.
New retailA new piece with a brand on it, in a shop. The top rung, and the one every listing quotes at you.

The gap between the floor and the top rung pays for the setting, the design, the brand, the shop and the risk. Nothing about it is illegitimate. The point is knowing how big it is before you agree to pay it.
